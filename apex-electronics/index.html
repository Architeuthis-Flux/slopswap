These notes cover 22 photos taken on the shelves at Apex Electronics. Each numbered tag on a photo links to a short note on what the thing is and what it does. Everything was worked out from the photos alone. Nothing was opened or powered, so treat the notes as a starting point and correct anything that is wrong.

Broadcast cameras

The wire shelves with television cameras: one row of 1990s studio heads and four portable cameras from the 1980s. One of the portables carries a Los Angeles station's asset tag.

BTS LDK 9 studio camera heads

Broadcast studio camera, 1990–91 · about six on the shelf

Read off the nameplate
What it is
Studio camera heads from BTS, the Bosch–Philips broadcast joint venture. The side panel carries the BTS logo and a large 9, and the rear panel is marked LDK 9. The photos show the operator's end: lit buttons for the filter wheels and set-up, plus intercom and tally connectors.
What it does
Three CCD sensors behind a colour-splitting prism turn the lens image into broadcast video. A head does not put out a finished picture by itself. Video, power, intercom and control all travel over one triax cable to a base station in the control room, so each head needs its base station to work. BTS billed the LDK 9 as the first frame-transfer CCD studio camera.

Sony DXC-3000A

3-chip CCD colour video camera, mid-1980s

Read off the nameplate
What it is
Marked DXC-3000A and 3CCD on the side. It was one of the first professional cameras to use three CCD chips in place of pickup tubes. Sony's brochure gives three 2/3-inch sensors, 560 TV lines and 58 dB signal-to-noise.
What it does
A field and studio camera head with no recorder built in. Composite video comes out on a BNC connector, the microphone plugs into the XLR socket on the side, and it runs from 12 V DC through the 4-pin XLR socket underneath. The brochure says it starts in about three seconds with no warm-up.

Same DXC-3000 family

What it is
A second Sony 3-chip body with a broadcast zoom lens still mounted. The top handle is gone and a threaded rod runs through the top cover. The side plate lists the filter wheel positions: 3200 K, 5600 K + 1/8 ND and 5600 K.
What it does
It works the same way as the DXC-3000A. The small panel under the lens mount has the gain, colour bars, white balance and power switches. The VF PREHEAT position on the power switch warms up the viewfinder's small picture tube.

JVC BY-110

Three-tube colour camera, tagged July 1984

Read off the nameplate
What it is
A complete BY-110 with its own servo zoom lens, lens hood and viewfinder. It uses three 1/2-inch Saticon pickup tubes behind an f/1.4 prism. JVC rated it at 600 lines and 54 dB.
What it does
A news and industrial camera from just before CCDs took over. Each tube scans one colour of the image with an electron beam. An auto-shift switch lines the red and blue pictures up with the green one and stores the result in battery-backed memory. It runs on 12 V at about 1 A, and video leaves on the 14-pin connector that went to the recorder.

Handle with care Pickup tubes burn. Keep the lens capped and never leave it aimed at the sun or a bright lamp.

Hitachi Z-31 Computacam

Three-tube colour camera (FP-Z31), mid-1980s

Read off the nameplate
What it is
Marked COMPUTACAM Z31 and HITACHI on the side. The FP-Z31 uses three 2/3-inch Saticon tubes. Hitachi's manual gives 750 lines and 58 dB.
What it does
A portable broadcast and industrial camera. The Computacam name refers to its microcomputer, which runs automatic white balance, black balance, centring and set-up so the three tube pictures stay registered without a technician. It runs on 12 V DC at about 20 W and puts out composite video.

Handle with care Pickup tubes burn. Keep the lens capped and out of direct sun.

Stage lighting

Two automated spotlights from a touring or theatre rig, opened up.

Moving-head stage lights

Automated arc-lamp spots, two with covers off

Recognised by shape
What it is
Two large moving-head fixtures lying on their sides. The open one shows the optical train: fans, lens carriages on rails, wheels and a driver board. The taped one behind it has the wide flared front lens of a Vari-Lite VL3000-type spot. The maker's label will confirm.
What it does
A discharge lamp sits at the back of the head. In front of it, stepper motors turn colour wheels and pattern wheels, slide the zoom and focus lenses and close an iris, all commanded over DMX from the lighting desk. The yoke that holds the head pans and tilts it.

Handle with care Leave any arc lamp in its housing. The lamps are under pressure and the igniter makes multi-kilovolt pulses.

16-button desk telephone

Looks like an AUTOVON set

Recognised by shape
What it is
A grey push-button desk phone whose keypad is four columns wide instead of three.
What it does
AUTOVON was the US military's worldwide telephone network, in service from 1966 until the early 1990s. The extra column of keys set a call's precedence: Priority, Immediate, Flash and Flash Override. A higher-precedence call could bump a lower one off a busy trunk. If the fourth column is marked FO, F, I and P, that is what this phone is.

Fluke 895A

DC differential voltmeter

Read off the nameplate
What it is
Labelled 895A DC DIFFERENTIAL VOLTMETER. A row of five readout dials runs across the middle of the panel.
What it does
A precision voltmeter that works by balancing. The five dials set a Kelvin-Varley divider that taps a known fraction of an internal 1100 V reference. You turn them until the meter reads zero, then read the voltage off the dials. It measures 0 to ±1100 V to about 0.0025%, and at null it draws no current from the circuit under test. It also works as a megohmmeter.

JEOL vacuum control rack

Ultra-high-vacuum pump controls

Read off the nameplate
What it is
JEOL across the top. The panels are labelled TMP/BAKE UNIT, a Pfeiffer Balzers TCP turbo-pump supply, VACUUM CONTROL UNIT with a mimic diagram, two SIP POWER SUPPLY panels and a titanium sublimation pump controller.
What it does
It runs the pumps of an ultra-high-vacuum instrument. The turbo pump takes the chamber down, timers run the bake-out heaters, and then sputter ion pumps and a titanium sublimation pump hold the vacuum. That pump set is typical of surface-analysis equipment such as Auger or photoelectron spectrometers.

Handle with care Ion pump supplies put out several kilovolts.

For the BC-375 and BC-191, three stacked

Recognised by shape
What it is
Three black panels, each with a white calibration chart in the middle and large dials. They match the plug-in tuning units of the BC-375 aircraft transmitter and its ground twin, the BC-191.

Each unit holds the coils and capacitors for one frequency band and slides into the front of the transmitter. Changing band meant swapping units. The TU-5, for example, covers 1.5 to 3 MHz.

Synchro heading test panel

Two-speed synchro simulator

Read off the nameplate
What it is
A pale grey panel marked HEADING (DEGREES) and SERVO FUNCTIONS. The jack columns are headed 1X CDX, 36X CDX, 1X CX and 36X CX, with rows S1 to S3 and R1 to R3.
What it does
Synchros send a shaft angle down wires as three AC voltages. This panel generates or breaks out those signals for a compass or heading system so it can be tested on the bench. 1X is the coarse synchro and 36X the fine one. CX is a control transmitter and CDX a differential transmitter.

Seat-reservation terminal

Read off the nameplate
What it is
A sloped console of square keys. The columns are headed SEAT TYPE, DATE, PERF and EVENT, with month keys from JAN to DEC. The lamps along the top read SEATS AVAIL and MAY BE AVAIL B.O.
What it does
An agent's terminal from an early computerized ticketing network. The clerk keyed in the event, performance, date and seat type. A central computer answered by lighting a lamp, and available seats were printed as tickets on the spot. Ticketron began selling this way in 1967, and a Los Angeles rival, Computicket, folded in 1970. It is the most unusual thing in these photos.

EG&G instrument, vacuum hardware and high voltage

One rack holds stainless vacuum plumbing, high-voltage parts and a long olive instrument lettered EG&G that is not yet pinned down.

EG&G instrument, serial 44

Not yet identified

Not identified
What it is
Hand-lettered “EG&G INC / C?-3B / SER. NO. 44”. The second character could be a T or an F. The body is a long olive fibreglass tube about the width of a dinner plate, with a black end cap and a recessed round window. A white cable enters a slot near that end. At the other end, in line, are a split aluminium clamp, a cross fitting with open side ports, a drum bolted together in two halves, a second cross fitting, another clamp and a small can with a wire harness.
What it does
Two readings fit. If the lettering is CT-3B, it may be an EG&G Model CT/3 electromagnetic ocean current meter. A NOAA test report from 1975 describes the CT/3 as a self-orienting meter with excitation coils and electrodes, a flux-gate compass and a cassette recorder, and fibreglass suits that job because it is non-magnetic and does not corrode. The other reading is a test-range optical instrument. EG&G photographed and measured US nuclear tests, and a long tube with a front window, a coil and a detector can would fit a telescope with a magnetic shutter or a photomultiplier. To tell them apart, shine a light into the front window and look for a lens, and check whether the side ports are open or hold glass.

Handle with care

  • Luminous dials. Treat glow-in-the-dark markings on WWII-era radios and instruments as radium paint. Leave those dials sealed and do not scrape or sand them.
  • Oil-filled cans. Capacitors and transformers made before 1979 may contain PCB oil unless the label says otherwise.
  • Big capacitors. They can hold or recover a charge for years. Keep the terminals shorted with a wire.
  • Camera tubes. Tube cameras are damaged by bright light even when switched off. Keep lenses capped and out of direct sun.
  • Arc lamps and X-ray tubes. Leave stage-light lamps in their housings, and leave the X-ray unit's tube and high-voltage tank sealed.
