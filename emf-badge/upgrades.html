An earlier survey, kept for reference. It was written on 10 November 2026, before anything was simulated. The chopper of Options C and E has since been worked through on the badge's own two cells, with a 74HC4053 in place of the 74HC4066, and priced for production: see A tuning knob for the EMF Explorer Badge. The frequency dividers, the automatic tuner and the single-AA supply are covered only here.

Can a 7400-series counter bring higher frequencies into hearing?

Yes. A 74HC4060 or 74HC4040 does it for about a dollar, and it needs no new pickup. Fields up to a few hundred kHz are already present at the op-amp output, attenuated by the 100 pF feedback capacitors.

Two limits come with any counter. It keeps pitch and throws away loudness, and it follows only the strongest signal. A chopper mixer costs about the same and keeps loudness and texture. Breadboard both before choosing.

Will a charge pump be quieter than the boost converter, on one AA?

It should be, because there is no inductor radiating into the pickups. The hard part is starting from 0.9 to 1.5 V. The LT1054 needs 3.5 V and the 7660 family needs 1.5 V, so neither runs on a part-used cell.

TI's TPS60310 does: 0.9 to 1.8 V in, about 700 kHz, five 1 µF capacitors, $1.29 at 1k. Treat its 2 × VIN output as a second cell stacked on the first and the rails come out at ±Vcell, the same as two AAAs give today. The LT1054s are still useful for a bench test from 4.5 V.

Can it keep loudness, with a knob or automatically?

Yes to both. A knob that sets the frequency shift is what the chopper already is. Option E widens its range and adds a comb mode that folds the whole band into hearing at once. For the dividers, one more 74HC4066 puts the field strength back onto the divided tone (Option D).

For automatic, Option F steers an audio oscillator from the source frequency, so any source glides into a range of a couple of octaves. It is the least proven circuit on this page.

Two facts from this design shape both upgrades.

The ultrasonic signal is already at the op-amp output. Each pickup feeds an inverting stage through 100 Ω, so the coil works into a near short circuit. Above roughly 500 Hz the coil's reactance is larger than its resistance, the coil current follows the field strength, and the stage behaves as a transimpedance amplifier. Its bandwidth is set by the feedback network: 100 kΩ with 100 pF rolls off at 16 kHz. Fields above that still come through at reduced gain.

The short-circuit loading also keeps the coil's self-capacitance out of play, so the 100 kHz self-resonance on the datasheet is a soft limit. I expect usable signal to a few hundred kHz. The rails only have to be ±1 V at a few milliamps. The NJM2115 is specified down to ±1.0 V and draws about 3.5 mA. That is why two AAAs last so long, and it sets a low bar for a single-AA supply.

Part 1

Hearing above 20 kHz

A ripple counter halves the frequency at every stage, so each tap sits one octave below the last and the output is a square wave. Bat detectors have used this for decades under the name frequency division, and their documented limits apply here: amplitude information is lost, and only the dominant signal is converted (Bat Conservation and Management, a bat-detector design site).

Three rules for either divider

Tap one channel and feed the other.
If the divided square wave is summed into the same op-amp it was tapped from, its harmonics above 16 kHz go round again and retrigger the counter. Tap channel A and inject into channel B, which puts the ultrasonic band in one ear. To keep it in both ears, low-pass the square wave with two RC poles near 2 kHz first.
Give the squaring stage hysteresis.
Without it the counter divides noise whenever no signal is present. The hysteresis level is the squelch setting.
Power the logic from 2 to 6 V.
On today's badge, run it between V− and V+ (3 V new, 2 V at end of life, where 74HCU04 gain fades). On the single-AA supply in Part 2, use the regulated 3.3 V output. VDD and VSS in the figures mean those two rails.
Option A

74HCU04 + 74HC4040

Two chips. Most gain, adjustable squelch.

How it works

Op-amp A's output goes through 220 pF into the first 74HCU04 inverter. A 1 MΩ feedback resistor biases each unbuffered inverter into its linear region, where the datasheet's application note gives about ×20 voltage gain and a 5 MHz gain-bandwidth product. Two stages give roughly ×400, less at lower supply voltage.

Each stage looks like about 48 kΩ at its input, so the two 220 pF capacitors form high-pass corners near 15 kHz that keep ordinary audio from clocking the counter. Two more inverters with 10 kΩ and 220 kΩ make a Schmitt trigger with about 150 mV of hysteresis. Referred back through the gain, that is a squelch threshold near 0.4 mV at the op-amp output. Lower R4 to raise it.

Good at

  • Staying silent until a real ultrasonic field shows up.
  • Sensitivity. It has the most gain of the three options.
  • Through-hole kits. Both chips come in DIP.

Watch for

  • Supply current. A linear-biased inverter conducts continuously, on the order of a milliamp per stage at 3 V. Measure it and give this section its own switch.
  • Self-oscillation. A gain of 400 a few centimetres from two pickup coils will oscillate if the squared signal runs near them. Put U1 and U2 at the far end of the board.
  • Strong signals just below 15 kHz. They leak through the high-pass and get divided too.
  • Twelve taps is more than the stock coils need. A 14-pin 74HC4024 would do.

Option B

74HC4060 and one transistor

Fewest parts. No squelch as drawn.

How it works

The 74HC4060's clock input (RS, pin 11) is the input of an on-chip inverter meant for an RC or crystal oscillator, and the datasheet allows driving it from an external signal instead. A 2.2 MΩ resistor from RTC (pin 10) back to RS holds that inverter at its threshold, so it does the squaring and no separate stage is needed.

One 2N3904 ahead of it adds about ×50. With 10 kΩ in the collector and 1 MΩ from collector to base it idles near 0.2 mA on 3.3 V. The first tap is ÷16, which is the lowest ratio this band needs.

Good at

  • Part count: one 16-pin chip, one transistor and a handful of passives.
  • Drawing less current than Option A.
  • A first breadboard test of whether divided sound is worth having.

Watch for

  • No hysteresis. With no signal the input stage amplifies its own noise and the counter divides it: a hiss on ÷16, a slow rumble on higher taps. Some builders will like that.
  • The dashed 10 MΩ from CTC (pin 9) to RS adds positive feedback and should give a small dead band. I have not tested it.
  • Less gain than Option A, so it needs a stronger field.

Option C

Chopper mixer: 74HC4066 + 74HC14

A tunable heterodyne. Keeps loudness and texture.

How it works

This one works at the input instead of the output. A 74HC4066 switch sits between each coil and its 100 Ω resistor and is driven by a square wave from a 74HC14 RC oscillator. Chopping the coil current multiplies it by that square wave, so a field at one frequency produces a component at the difference between it and the oscillator frequency. The existing amplifier treats that difference like any other audio.

A second switch, driven in the opposite phase, grounds the coil while the first is open, so the coil always works into a low impedance. With 470 pF and 4.7 kΩ to 105 kΩ the oscillator covers roughly 20 to 450 kHz, depending on the chip's thresholds. Close SW2 and the oscillator stops with the series switch held on. The badge then behaves as it does today.

Good at

  • Keeping loudness. Move closer and it gets louder, and modulated sources keep their rhythm.
  • Simplicity. No extra gain and no path from output back to input.
  • Reaching past the 16 kHz roll-off, because the conversion happens ahead of the amplifier.
  • Play value. The tuning knob behaves like an instrument.

Watch for

  • It hears a window about 15 kHz either side of the oscillator frequency, with fainter windows near three and five times that. You have to tune.
  • With the oscillator running, ordinary audio still passes at half level.
  • Switch charge injection puts some oscillator signal into the amplifier. It is above hearing over most of the dial. Listen for whistles near the low end.
  • 74HC4066 on-resistance climbs as the supply falls. Run it from 3.3 V if you can.

Part 1, continued

Keeping loudness, with a knob or automatically

Loudness survives only if the field strength travels through a linear path. A mixer is linear by nature, which is why the chopper in Option C already keeps it. A divider is not, so the amplitude has to be measured separately and put back onto the divided tone.

The two families also sound different. Dividing keeps frequency ratios: a source that wobbles by 2% still wobbles by 2%, and the whole band is audible at once. Mixing keeps frequency differences: a 100 kHz source that wobbles by 2 kHz becomes a 1 kHz tone that wobbles by 2 kHz. Mixing zooms in on texture, and it has to be tuned.

Option E · the knob

Pot-tuned chopper with a comb mode

One pot sets the shift. Loudness and texture kept. Two chips.

How it works

This is Option C with two changes that let one pot cover the whole band. The oscillator runs lower, about 18 to 97 kHz, and its harmonics do the rest: a square wave at 50 kHz also converts fields near 150 and 250 kHz, at a third and a fifth of the gain.

A three-position switch then chooses what drives the switches. NORMAL holds the series switch on. TUNED uses the square wave. COMB uses a pulse about 3 µs wide, which converts every harmonic of the oscillator about equally, so fields across the band fold into hearing together. In COMB the pot stops being a tuning dial and becomes a pitch trim: every source is audible somewhere, and the knob slides it to a comfortable note.

Good at

  • Being simple to use: one knob and one switch.
  • Loudness and texture, in both modes.
  • Needing no gain stages, squelch or divider.

Watch for

  • COMB is quieter. Conversion gain is roughly the pulse's duty cycle, about −20 dB with a 3 µs pulse at 30 kHz, and ordinary audio drops with it. It also folds in noise from every band.
  • Pulse width sets the reach. Conversion fades toward 1 ÷ pulse width, about 330 kHz for 3 µs. A smaller C2 reaches higher and is quieter still.
  • On one side of each harmonic the pitch moves backwards, so turning the knob up can lower the note.
  • Frequency goes as 1/R, so a linear pot crowds one end of the dial. A reverse-log taper spreads it.
  • The oscillator keeps running in NORMAL. If the pickups hear it, use a second pole on SW3 to stop it.

Option D

Loudness stage for the dividers

One more chip on Option A or B. The divided tone follows field strength.

How it works

Two 74HC4066 switches in series sit between the linear ultrasonic signal and channel B. The first is driven by the squared signal, so it passes one polarity of each cycle. That makes it a synchronous rectifier, and unlike a diode it stays linear down to the noise. The second is driven by the selected divider tap, so the rectified signal is switched on and off at the divided frequency.

R7 and C5 smooth away the ultrasonic ripple. What remains is the divided tone with a height proportional to the field. With these values a 100 kHz field should come out within a few dB of an audio-band field of the same strength. The stage replaces C3, RV1 and R5 in Fig. A.

Good at

  • Loudness that tracks field strength, so moving closer gets louder.
  • Muting itself when the signal goes away.
  • Using half of one chip. The other two switches are spare.

Watch for

  • The squared signal lags the linear one because of U1B's bandwidth. By my estimate loudness reads under 1 dB low at 100 kHz and about 4 dB low at 250 kHz.
  • Charge injection from the second switch leaves a faint constant tone under weak signals.
  • U1A clips near 1 V peak to peak, which leaves about 35 dB between squelch and clipping.
  • With Option B, take the linear signal from Q1's collector and the squared signal from CTC (pin 9).

Option F

Pitch follower: tach and VCO

Automatic. Any source glides into a range of a couple of octaves. Least proven.

How it works

The squared signal drives a diode pump, which is a frequency-to-voltage converter. Its output rises with frequency and flattens above about 200 kHz, so 20 kHz to 1 MHz is squeezed into a small voltage range. That voltage steers the audio-rate oscillator in a 74HC4046, and the oscillator takes the place of the divider tap in Fig. D.

Pitch then follows source frequency on a compressed scale and loudness follows field strength. C7 sets how fast the pitch glides to a new source, about 40 ms with 1 µF and proportionally slower with more. RV2 moves the whole range up or down.

Good at

  • Never being out of range. There is nothing to select or tune.
  • The slow shift you described. It is one capacitor.
  • Keeping order: a higher source still gives a higher note.

Watch for

  • It is a synthesised tone. Texture survives only as loudness changes and as pitch wobble slower than the glide.
  • The 74HC4046 oscillator needs 3 V or more, and at 3 V it responds linearly only between 1.1 and 1.9 V on its control pin. R12 to R14 fit the tach into that window. Expect to trim them. Two AAAs cannot run it.
  • R11, RV2 and C8 are scaled from one datasheet point. The real pitch range will need adjusting.
  • It needs Option A's front end and Fig. D, so four chips in all.

Other automatic routes

A stepping auto-ranger. A 74HC151 multiplexer picks the divider tap, a 74HC191 up/down counter holds the range, and a window comparator on a tach of the output steps the counter a few times a second until the tone sits between about 400 Hz and 2.5 kHz. It keeps exact division and gives band LEDs for free. It also costs three more chips on top of Options A and D, and the pitch arrives in octave jumps.

A small microcontroller as the tuner. It times the squared signal with a capture input and either picks the tap or generates the chopper's oscillator, slewing as slowly as you like. The audio path stays analog. A PIC16F18313 comes in 8-pin DIP, runs from 2.3 V and has a numerically controlled oscillator (datasheet). That matters because a plain 48 MHz timer can only place a 500 kHz output in 5 kHz steps. A CH32V003 costs under $0.10 and is surface-mount only. The costs are firmware to write and flash, and one more digital part for the pickups to hear. I have not priced or drawn either route.

Part 2

Split rails from one AA

A charge pump stores no energy in a magnetic field, so its stray field is far smaller than a boost inductor's. Its switching currents still flow in loops, so the capacitors belong tight against the IC and the pump belongs away from the pickups.

The hard part is input voltage. An alkaline AA starts near 1.5 V and delivers most of its energy between 1.3 V and 1.0 V, so the first stage has to start and run at about 0.9 V.

One guess about the present design: if its boost converter is a PFM or pulse-skipping type, that is probably what the pickups hear. At a few milliamps of load those converters fire in bursts at audio rates. A fixed-frequency converter moves the noise above hearing, which is what Plan B below relies on.

Option 1 · start here

TPS60310 as a second cell

One IC and five capacitors. Rails of ±Vcell, as the badge has now.

How it works

The TPS60310's first stage is a plain voltage doubler, and its output is brought out as OUT1: twice the input, up to 40 mA, with about 4 Ω of source impedance. Call the cell's positive terminal ground. The cell's negative terminal is then V− and OUT1 is V+, which gives +1.5 and −1.5 V on a new cell and ±1.0 V near the end.

That is what the NJM2115 sees today, so the amplifier needs no changes. The second stage gives a regulated 3.3 V, measured from V−, for an LED and for the Part 1 logic.

Cost

The cell supplies about twice the OUT1 load, so the amplifier alone draws 7 to 10 mA from the AA. My estimate from datasheet currents is a couple of hundred hours on an alkaline cell. One AA holder in place of two AAA holders saves about $0.54 a board at 1k (Keystone 2460 at $0.76 against two Keystone 2466 at $0.65), which covers part of the IC.

Watch for

  • Tie SNOOZE high. Snooze mode regulates in bursts at light load.
  • The datasheet lists a "LinSkip switching threshold" of 7.5 mA. I read that as the load below which the regulated stage changes to pulse-skipping, which can land in the audio band. Power the op-amp from OUT1 and put a scope on OUT1 to see how much skip ripple reaches it.
  • VIN is limited to 1.8 V. Alkaline and NiMH cells are fine. A 1.5 V lithium primary cell can sit close to that limit when new.
  • The package is a 10-pin VSSOP at 0.5 mm pitch. Have the board house fit it and leave the through-hole parts for the kit builder.
  • Ground is the cell's positive terminal. That is fine for a floating battery device and worth a note on the schematic.

More headroom, about a third of the battery life.

Option 3 · bench test

LT1054 inverter from the parts you have

Not a single-AA supply. It answers whether the pickups can hear a charge pump at all.

How it works

The LT1054 needs 3.5 V, so it cannot be the production supply. It can still run an existing badge from three AA cells as a plain inverter: V+ is 4.5 V and V− is about −4.2 V, inside the NJM2115's ±7 V limit. Use cells and not USB power, so the pump is the only thing switching.

At 25 kHz, with bipolar switches and larger capacitors, it is a pessimistic stand-in for the audio band. If it is quiet at the distance the board can give it, a 700 kHz pump moving a few milliamps should be too.

Procedure

  1. Listen to the badge on its two AAAs for a baseline.
  2. Power it from this rig with the LT1054 10 cm from the pickups.
  3. Bring the pump closer until you hear it. Note the distance and which coil orientation is worst.
  4. With a Part 1 prototype attached, repeat. A divider on ÷16 turns the 25 kHz pump into a 1.56 kHz tone, which makes any coupling obvious.

The badge's LED resistor was chosen for 3 V. At 8.7 V across the rails the LED current rises to several milliamps, which is fine for a test.

Plan B

Fixed-frequency boost with a diode pump

Cheapest silicon. Still has an inductor.

How it works

If the charge pump's price or 20 mA limit does not fit, the MCP1640B is the PWM-only version of the MCP1640. It runs at a constant 500 kHz, starts at 0.65 V typical, and never drops into audio-rate bursts. A capacitor and two Schottky diodes on its switch node make the negative rail for a few cents.

Use a shielded inductor, put it at the far end of the board, and turn its axis at right angles to the pickups.

Watch for

  • It is still a coil carrying switching current next to a field sensor. 500 kHz is outside the audio path and inside the Part 1 band.
  • PWM-only operation costs light-load efficiency. Check the no-load input current in the datasheet against the battery life you want.
  • The negative rail is unregulated and about 0.6 V short of the positive one.
  • The feedback divider values are the datasheet's 3.3 V example. Confirm them against the current revision.

Putting them together

How the two upgrades interact

Once the badge can hear above 20 kHz, its own power supply is inside the band it listens to. If the supply's switching reaches the ultrasonic path above the squelch threshold, the divider turns it into a steady tone.

This is useful as well as a hazard. A divider prototype is the most sensitive instrument you have for laying out the supply: move the pump around and listen. In chopper mode the supply appears as a fixed carrier at one dial position, which is easy to recognise and ignore.

The two parts also share a rail. The TPS60310's regulated 3.3 V output gives the logic a constant supply, which keeps the 74HCU04's gain and the 74HC4066's on-resistance steady over the life of the cell.

Suggested order on the bench

  1. Breadboard the chopper as Option E on a stock badge, with the logic across the two AAAs. Try TUNED and COMB on the same sources.
  2. Breadboard Option B beside it. Tap pin 1, feed pin 6, and compare divided sound with mixed sound by ear.
  3. If the divider earns its place, add the Option D loudness stage.
  4. Run the LT1054 test. Note how close the pump can sit before any prototype hears it.
  5. Put a TPS60310 on a VSSOP-10 breakout, build Option 1, and repeat the test with the pump at 700 kHz. Scope OUT1 for ripple at audio rates.
  6. Lay out the board with the pump and the logic at the end farthest from the pickups.
