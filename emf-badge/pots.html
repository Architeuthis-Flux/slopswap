Which pot to buy

The knob is the largest single cost in the badge's tuning circuit. Here is every kind of pot that works, with prices.

Design notes · 13 November 2026 · part of A tuning knob for the EMF Explorer Badge

The oscillator uses the pot as a plain variable resistor, on two of its three pins, so almost anything whose resistance changes will tune it. That leaves the choice to price and feel, and the prices found run from four cents to four dollars.

Any value from 10 kΩ to 100 kΩ

The cheap parts mostly come in 10 kΩ. The frequency depends on the pot's resistance times the capacitor, so a smaller pot takes a larger capacitor and a smaller fixed resistor, and the range stays where it was.

Simulated with the same 74HC14 model as the main page (badge_pots.py), which gives the gate 150 Ω of output resistance. That resistance is in series with the fixed resistor, so it starts to count at 1 kΩ: with 400 Ω, which is plausible on tired cells, the top of the 10 kΩ range falls to about 150 kHz. Cheap pots are ±20 to ±30 %, and the bottom of the range moves by as much.

Trimmers: the cheapest end

A 100 kΩ carbon trimmer at about four cents is the floor. It drops into the schematic as drawn and takes the recommended build from $0.50 to $0.22. The cost is in use. A trimmer is turned through a slot, and it is built to be set a few times: Bourns rates its 3362 trimmer for 200 cycles of rotation. A badge that is played like an instrument could use that up in a few afternoons. A trimmer makes sense if the knob is closer to a preset than an instrument.

The cheapest part that is a real knob is the 16 mm carbon shaft pot at about 14 cents, if it can be had and the badge has room for it. After it come the slide pot at 30 cents and the Alps shaft pot at 32 cents.

Position sensors as sliders

LCSC files a second family of sliders under position sensors and not under potentiometers. A resistive linear position sensor is a slide pot made for a mechanism to move instead of a finger: a 10 kΩ track, a short lever and through-hole pins. It tunes this circuit like any other 10 kΩ pot, and it can undercut the brand-name thumbwheels and slide pots in the same catalogue: the Alps RDC1022A05 slider is from $0.76, where a Bourns slide pot is from $1.36 and Bourns thumbwheels start at $1.17, and the Alps rotary sensor at $0.41 is under the Alps thumbwheel at $0.53. The low-cost YTL slide pot is still cheaper than any of them.

The drawback is feel. These parts slide with very little resistance (Alps rates the RDC10 series at 0.25 N at most), so they are not as pleasant under a thumb as a proper slide pot. In their favour, the same datasheet rates them for 200,000 strokes. They are linear only, so the top of the dial is crowded; the narrower 10 kΩ row in the first table evens that out.
