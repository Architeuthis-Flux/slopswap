Breadboard prototype and bench notes

How the badge's tuning chopper was prototyped on a breadboard at 5 V from parts on hand, with a Jumperless V5 as clock, supply and test bench.

Bench notes · 10 and 11 November 2026 · published here 13 November 2026

Read this first

These are working notes, written to one person at a bench. Their numbers are simulation, not measurement. The circuit to make is on the main page, which came later and runs on the badge's own two cells.

Three things here have since changed. The 1 µF series capacitor, C1, costs bass in normal listening (about 11 dB at 120 Hz in simulation); 10 µF brings that within about 2 dB, and on the badge's own ±1.5 V the capacitor is not needed at all. The production switch is a 74HC4053, not the 74HC4066 named here. And a CMOS 555 works as the oscillator on the badge's 3 V; the note here against the NE555 is about the bipolar part.

The script, the simulation files and the handoff notes that the text mentions are not published here. Two tabs of the original guide, Wiring and substitutes and Simulations, are not on this page yet; they are in the original on claude.ai.

EMF Badge Chopper Test on a Jumperless V5

Written 10 November 2026

What this tests

The Jumperless stands in for the 74HC14 oscillator and one discrete MOSFET stands in for the 74HC4066. Every chopper setting on the options page can then be heard on the real badge. The extra parts are one transistor, one capacitor and one resistor.

On the options pageHow the Jumperless does itMode in the script

Tuned chopper, Options C and E50% square wave on GPIO 1, 22 to 300 kHz, tuned with the wheelTUNED
Comb mode, Option ESame oscillator, but the signal passes only for a short pulse each cycle. Pulse width is adjustableCOMB
The slow automatic shift you asked forThe oscillator glides across the band by itself, or scans and parks on the strongest sourceSWEEP, SEEK

What it cannot test. Option D, the loudness stage for the dividers, and the 74HC4046 circuit of Option F. SEEK and TRACK show what automatic tuning feels like, but they are software. DIVIDE and TRACK need your CD4049; everything else needs only the transistor.

Where the bodge differs from the real circuit. These matter when you judge what you hear.

  • Shunt only. The real circuit has a series switch and a shunt switch. This has only the shunt, so a few percent of the signal leaks through when it should be off.
  • Bass leaks. The capacitor in series with the MOSFET lets sound below about 1 kHz through unchopped. Hum stays audible in every mode.
  • More hiss. While the MOSFET is on, the op-amp sees 100 Ω to ground and amplifies its own noise more. The 74HC4066 version opens the series switch during that phase and is quieter.
  • Stepped tuning. The oscillator is a divider from the 150 MHz system clock. Steps are 6 Hz at 30 kHz, 67 Hz at 100 kHz and 270 Hz at 200 kHz. An RC oscillator is continuous.

Parts and hookup

One MOSFET goes from the coil side of the badge's 100 Ω input resistor to ground, through a capacitor. Three clip leads reach the badge: that junction, AGND and the op-amp output.

With the gate low, the coil current flows through the 100 Ω into the amplifier as it does today. With the gate high, it flows through C1 and Q1 to ground instead. R2 and C2 are only needed for the level meter, recording and the speaker.

An NPN in place of Q1. A 2N3904, PN2222 or similar small switching transistor works, because C1 blocks its saturation offset. Put the collector in row 12, the base in row 13 and the emitter in row 14. Set ROW_GATE = 16 and put 1 kΩ from row 16 to row 13. It turns off roughly 0.2 to 0.5 µs late, so treat COMB pulses under 1 µs as unreliable. This version is untested too.

Where to clip on the badge

  1. Junction. The end of a 100 Ω input resistor that goes to its pickup coil, not the end that goes to the op-amp. A meter reads under 1 Ω from it to one coil leg.
  2. AGND. The battery midpoint.
  3. Audio. The op-amp output for the same channel. The NJM2115 pairs are pin 2 in with pin 1 out, and pin 6 in with pin 7 out. Follow the resistor to see which pair you are on.

On the breadboard

  • Junction lead to row 10. C1 from row 10 to row 12.
  • Q1 with its drain in row 12, gate in row 13 and source in row 14. A BS270 reads drain, gate, source with the flat side facing you. A 2N7000 is the mirror image.
  • A jumper wire from row 14 to the GND rail. Do not use connect() for this one. A crosspoint adds about 40 Ω and the shunt has to be a few ohms.
  • R1 from row 13 to the GND rail. AGND lead to the GND rail.
  • Audio lead to row 18. R2 from row 18 to row 20. C2 from row 20 to the GND rail.

Twist the junction and AGND leads together and keep them short. Keep the badge 20 cm or more from the Jumperless, whose LEDs and converters are an EMF source themselves. That also makes the Jumperless a handy first thing to listen to.

Loading and running

The script is one file, emf_chopper.py. Set the row numbers at the top, copy it to the board, and start it from the terminal or the wheel.

  1. Settings. Check the block headed settings at the top of the file. Only ROW_GATE is required. ROW_AUDIO = 20 switches on the level meter and SEEK. Every other feature is off until you give it a row.
  2. Copy. Press U in the Jumperless terminal and the board mounts as a USB drive. Put the file in python_scripts, then eject the drive.
  3. Run. Type /python_scripts/emf_chopper.py in the main terminal. From the wheel it is Click, then Files, then the file. From the Python REPL, exec(open('/python_scripts/emf_chopper.py').read()) does the same.
  4. Stop. Press q or Ctrl+Q. The script drives the gate low, so the badge is back to normal, and removes the connections it made.

The script clears whatever was connected to the rows it uses before it starts. It was written against firmware 5.7.11 and has not run on real hardware yet. The last section lists what that leaves open.

Listening

Headphones in the badge's own jack are the best way to hear this. The speaker and USB paths are quieter or coarser, and are there for sharing and recording.

PathSettingWhat to expect
Badge headphonesNoneFull quality. Nothing on the badge changes
Jumperless buffer to a speakerROW_SPEAKER = 6Unity gain through crosspoints. Faint on a bare 8 Ω speaker. A piezo disc or a powered speaker suits it better
USB audioUSB_AUDIO = TrueThe board appears as a microphone called JL Audio In. Fine for spectrograms, coarse for quiet sources

Speaker. Put the speaker between row 6 and the GND rail. The script routes the audio row into BUFFER_IN and BUFFER_OUT to row 6. BUFFER_IN is also the probe's analog line, so the probe is unpowered while the script runs. Unplug it or flip it to measure, or its LED supply loads the signal. You know this path better than I do. I only have the firmware source to go on.

USB audio.

  1. Set USB_AUDIO = True and run the script. The first run switches the microphone on and the serial port drops for about two seconds. Run the script again.
  2. Choose JL Audio In as the input in Audacity, QuickTime or a DAW, and record.
  3. Left is the chopped channel. Right is ROW_REF, if you wire it to the other op-amp output: the unchopped channel, for comparison.
  4. usb_audio_teardown() in the REPL hides the microphone again.

Two limits apply to the USB path. The ADC is 12 bits across about ±9 V, so one step is about 4.5 mV and a 100 mV badge signal gets only four or five bits. And the ADC samples at 48 kHz with no filter, so without R2 and C2 the chopper's own switching shows up as a whistle that moves as you tune. For a clean recording, a cable from the badge's jack into a line input will beat it.

With the CD4049

Two of its six inverters, biased as amplifiers, turn the badge's ultrasonic signal into a logic square wave. With that on a GPIO the Jumperless can divide it and count it, which adds DIVIDE and TRACK.

Each 1 MΩ resistor holds its inverter at the switching point, where it amplifies by roughly 20. The 1 nF capacitors keep hum and ordinary audio out. R7 and R8 bring the 5 V swing down to 3.3 V for the GPIO. Feed it from the channel you are not chopping, so the chopper cannot disturb its own frequency reference.

Settings. ROW_SQUARE = 50 and ROW_TONE = 60. The script puts 5 V on the top rail while it runs and restores it afterwards.

DIVIDE is Options A and B. A PIO program divides the squared signal by 4 to 512 and sends it out of row 60 as a 3.3 V square wave. Put a speaker from row 60 to the GND rail. For headphones, run 1 MΩ from row 60 to the inverting input of the chopped channel, the same injection Fig. A uses.

TRACK is the automatic mode. The script counts the source's frequency ten times a second, puts the oscillator just below it and follows slowly. A source therefore glides in and settles on a steady note, with loudness intact.

SourceNote it lands on
Below 15 kHzNot shifted. The chopper stops and the sound passes straight through
20 kHz400 Hz
80 kHz800 Hz
320 kHz1.6 kHz, reached on the oscillator's third harmonic

The wheel moves the whole scale up or down. TRACK_SPREAD = 0 gives every source the same note, and TRACK_GLIDE_S sets how slowly it follows.

What to expect from the squarer

  • Sensitivity. The GPIO's own input hysteresis is the squelch. I expect it to trip at a few millivolts of ultrasound at the op-amp output, but that depends on the chip. If it is deaf, add a third stage wired like the second. If it chatters on noise, raise R4 to 100 kΩ.
  • Hunting. The squarer follows whatever is loudest above about 3 kHz, including the chopper's own switching. If TRACK wanders up and down, that is the cause. Keep the squarer at the far end of the board from Q1.
  • Chip type. It has to be the unbuffered CD4049UB, which is the usual one. Keep it at 5 V. Biased this way it draws a few milliamps per gate and far more at higher voltages.

A real knob instead. Rewired, three of the inverters make the actual RC oscillator, for the true analog dial.

  • Join pin 6 to pin 5, and pin 4 to pin 3.
  • From pin 6, 4.7 kΩ in series with a 20 kΩ pot to a node X. 680 pF from pin 4 to X. 22 kΩ from X to pin 7.
  • Pin 2 is the output. Wire it to Q1's gate through 4.7 kΩ, and set ROW_GATE to an unused row first so GPIO 1 is not fighting it.

In simulation it covers about 24 to 100 kHz, and its 5 V swing turns Q1 on harder than the GPIO does. Build this or the squarer, not both. They use the same inverters.

Test plan

Work down the list in order. Each step proves one thing before the next step depends on it.

  1. NORMAL. The badge should sound as it did before the leads went on. If there is new hash, move it away from the Jumperless and twist the leads.
  2. SHUNT. Sound above about 1 kHz should drop to a few percent. If nothing changes, the junction clip is on the wrong node or Q1 is not turning on.
  3. Test tone at 40 kHz. Set ROW_TEST = 30, wire row 30 through 1 kΩ and the loop to the GND rail, and press x for 1.00k with the loop held near the coil. In NORMAL the badge should hear that buzz directly, which proves the loop, the leads and the audio path. Then press x until the display reads 40k. In TUNED, tune from 38 to 42 kHz. A clean tone should fall to zero at 40 kHz and rise again.
  4. Test tone at 100 kHz. Tune to 33.3 kHz. The tone should reappear, fainter. That is the third-harmonic window.
  5. Real sources in TUNED. Try a phone charger, a laptop supply, an LED bulb, an induction hob, a wireless charging pad and the Jumperless itself. Note where each is loudest.
  6. COMB on the same sources. Start at 3 µs and compare the level with TUNED. Widen the pulse until it is loud enough and note the width.
  7. SWEEP. Decide whether a whoop every few seconds tells you enough. Try 3 s and 15 s passes.
  8. SEEK, or TRACK with the CD4049. Move between two sources and time how long it takes to find the new one.
  9. DIAL, or the CD4049 oscillator. Tune to one source with each taper. Note how touchy the top of the dial is.
  10. Record one source in NORMAL, TUNED and COMB over USB audio, to compare later.

Carrying the results back

Four findings from the bench set the parts of the real circuit.

Two more things are worth judging while you listen.

  • The bodge might be the product. If shunt-only chopping sounds good enough, one SOT-23 MOSFET and a capacitor per channel replace the 74HC4066. The price is the extra hiss and the bass leak. Compare the two when a 4066 arrives.
  • Oscillator steadiness. The Jumperless oscillator is divided from a crystal and is steadier than any RC oscillator. Tones will sound cleaner here than they will from a 74HC14. The CD4049 oscillator shows how much an RC oscillator's jitter roughens the note.

Symptoms

  • Silence or heavy distortion in every mode. The op-amp output is against a rail. Check that C1 is in series with Q1's drain and is not shorted.
  • TUNED sounds the same as NORMAL. Nothing ultrasonic is in range, or Q1 is not switching. Use the test tone and the SHUNT check to tell which.
  • A whistle near the bottom of the tuning range. That is the oscillator's own switching. Raise LO_MIN.
  • Buzz in every mode. Hum picked up by the leads, which the bodge does not chop. Shorten and twist them.
  • The serial port vanished. Expected on the first run with USB_AUDIO = True. Reconnect and run the script again.
  • Probe LEDs dark. The speaker path has borrowed the buffer. They come back when the script stops.

Building the chopper for real

The first tabs use the Jumperless as a stand-in for the switch. This tab builds the switch out of a real part, with the Jumperless as clock, supply and test bench.

Where this fits

The badge hears 20 Hz to 20 kHz. The goal is a tunable window above that: a chopper slides a band from the 20 to 100 kHz region down into the audible range, and a knob sets where the band sits. Two parts make it. The switch is the mixer, and the oscillator is the dial. On these pages the Jumperless stands in for the oscillator, so the switch can be tested with a clock that is easy to change; the knob version, further down, replaces it with three inverters and a pot and needs no Jumperless at all.

The chip on the board is the CD4049UB hex inverter, not a 4066. The 74HC4066 is the switch the final version would use, and it is in the table at the end. The chopper itself needs one inverter, to give the shunt the opposite phase from the series switch. The rest of the chip has two possible jobs, and they are alternatives: three inverters as the RC oscillator (the knob), or two as the squarer from the first tab. The squarer is the feedback loop: it turns channel B's output into a square wave, the Jumperless counts it and parks the clock 1 kHz below, which is the TRACK mode. That is automatic tuning on top of the window, not the window itself, so it comes last.

Ten drawings follow: the schematic and the whole board, the switch rows close up, the two stand-ins for the second BS270 as a schematic and on the board, the knob version as a schematic, on the board and with its one NPN close up, and the CD4053 version as a schematic and on the board.

What changes when the switch is real

The first tabs put one MOSFET across the coil as a shunt and let the Jumperless pretend to be the chopper. That hears the effect, but it is not the circuit: the coil never opens, bass leaks through unchopped, and the mute is a few per cent short of silent. Option C on the options page is a series switch that opens the coil path, with a shunt that grounds the coil while the path is open.

This tab builds that from parts already on the bench: two BS270s, one inverter of the CD4049UB, and a handful of passives. No new ICs. The Jumperless stops being the switch and becomes the rest of the bench. It makes the clock, powers the inverter, listens through the audio tap and measures what it can.

It costs one solder joint on the badge. The coil end of channel A's 100 Ω input resistor has to be lifted, so the switch can sit between the coil and the amplifier. Left in place, the badge's own coil would stay wired to the amplifier around the switch.

The ETA7460 board

As far as I can find, the part is ETEK's ET7460H. Nothing called ETA7460 turns up in any catalogue I can reach, the ET7460 marking is sold on the kind of WLCSP breakout you describe, and ETEK's own pages and datasheet cover it. If your board says something else, tell me and I will look again.

It is a dual SPST switch made for routing speakers and power in phones, and its datasheet reads like one:

Not the chopper switch. At 40 kHz a half-cycle is 12.5 µs, so the switch would still be turning on when told to turn off. Even at 20 kHz, the bottom of the band, 15 of the 25 µs half-cycle go on turning on. And a gate big enough for 0.1 Ω carries nanocoulombs; the spikes it would put into the amplifier would be larger than the coil signal. Its strengths, 0.1 Ω and ±13 V, are for speakers, not for millivolts of coil current.

Where it would fit: anything slow. Option D's tone gate runs at 0.5 to 4 kHz, where 15 µs is 1 to 6 % of a half-cycle. A NORMAL/mute bypass switch. Routing the badge's headphone signal. Keep it for those.

To see it on the Jumperless, with no badge involved: VCC and EN to the 3.3 V rail, GND to GND, S1 from DAC 0 at 2 V, D1 through 1 kΩ to GND, IN1 from a GPIO making 50 % PWM, and ADC 1 on D1. Average a few hundred adc_get reads. At 500 Hz the average on D1 is about 1.0 V. Raise the PWM to 20 kHz and then 40 kHz and watch it fall, as the turn-on time eats the on half-cycle. A switch fit for the chopper reads 1.0 V at all three. How far it falls depends on how the datasheet defines its 15 µs, so the fall is the result, not its size.

The circuit

This is Option C's switch with two BS270s in place of the 74HC4066. M1 sits in series between the coil, node A, and the amplifier. M2 grounds node A while M1 is open, so the coil always works into a low impedance, as it does on the stock badge.

Three parts need a word.

C1 stays, and for a different reason than before. With the series switch, the op-amp's input offset would see 100 Ω to ground half the time and an open circuit the other half, and come out of the amplifier as a square wave at the LO frequency. C1 blocks the offset altogether. In simulation the output then sits within a few millivolts of zero in every mode, where the stock stage sits at 0.28 V.

C3 is new. M1's gate-to-source capacitance couples the LO straight into the amplifier's summing node, and the 100 pF in the feedback turns that charge into voltage: in simulation, 280 mV of 39 kHz at the output, three times what the shunt bodge leaked. Inaudible, but it eats half the headroom. The inverted drive is the same square wave upside down, so a small capacitor from it into node B cancels the charge. 15 pF brings the leak down to 48 mV in simulation. It is a trim: try 10, 15 and 22 pF.

RG1 and RG2 slow the gate edges a little and keep the GPIO and the inverter happy driving the gates through a crosspoint. Any value from 470 Ω to 2.2 kΩ works.

If you have only one BS270

Give it to M1. The series switch is the one that makes the mute and the tone: it has to pass millivolts through a few ohms and then block them completely, and the BS270's low threshold keeps it doing that from a 3.3 V gate. The shunt only has to hold node A near ground while M1 is open, so almost anything that switches will do, and the simulation says what each one costs.

Which NPN. Between a BC550C and an S9013, the BC550C. It is the smaller, faster die: fT about 250 MHz against 100 to 150 MHz, 2.5 pF collector-base capacitance, and 75 mV saturation at 10 mA, which is far more current than the coil ever asks of it. The S9013 is a 500 mA driver and brings nothing the shunt can use. Both are driven hundreds of times harder than they need to be, so both turn off a few hundred nanoseconds late; the smaller one less so. The BC550C reads C B E from the flat face, so it goes in the other way round from the 2N3904 in the drawing, with the collector still on row 9. The S9013 reads E B C and goes in like the 2N3904. RG2 stays 1 kΩ; with the BC550C's gain of 400 or more, 4.7 kΩ also works and shortens the turn-off a little.

What the Jumperless does now

Clock. GPIO 1 still makes the LO: PWM from 22 to 300 kHz, 50 % for TUNED, narrow pulses for COMB, swept for SWEEP and SEEK. The polarity flips, because the switch now passes when the gate is high: NORMAL is LO held high, the mute is LO held low, and a COMB pulse is a short high. That is a small change to the script, listed in the handoff.

Fan-out. The crossbar replaces two long jumpers: GPIO 1 reaches the inverter's input on row 28 as well as row 19, and the inverter's output on row 27 reaches the shunt's gate resistor on row 13.

Power. The bottom + rail at 3.3 V runs the CD4049. The script only touches the top rail, and only for the squarer, so for now set it by hand at the main prompt: > dac_set(BOTTOM_RAIL, 3.3, False). The handoff asks the code session to add a setting for it.

Listening. Your powered speaker on the badge's jack is the main ear. The audio tap on row 33 feeds the level meter, SEEK and USB audio as before.

Measuring. The Jumperless can read DC levels on any row, count a logic frequency with its PIO, estimate a resistance with the DAC and current sensor, and see audio-band tones through the tap. It cannot see the 40 kHz waveform or the gate-edge spikes: its ADC samples at 48 kHz. There is one way round that, untested: with the test tone off, run the LO at 47 kHz. Whatever LO leaks to the badge output aliases to 1 kHz in a 48 kHz sampler, and a second ADC channel clipped straight to the audio lead, ahead of R2, reads it at full size. Trim C3 for the smallest reading. If a real scope is within reach, use it instead.

The knob version

This is the tunable window with nothing but parts: the switch, three CD4049 inverters as an RC oscillator, and a pot. The Jumperless makes no clock. It still routes three links, sets the rail and listens, and once those links are wires it can go. The oscillator is the one from the first tab, unchanged; in simulation the 20 kΩ pot takes it from about 24 to 100 kHz, so anything from the low 20s to about 100 kHz can be brought down to a tone.

The pot and R9 set the frequency with C6; R10 only protects inverter C's input. Inverter A buffers the loop so the gates do not load it. The same pin 2 drives RG1 directly and inverter D, whose output drives RG2.

As built: M1 is the BS270 on rows 14 to 16, M2 is the BC550C on rows 7 to 9 behind C4, and the oscillator's 20 kΩ pot sits in the free space on rows 43 to 45, wiper on 44 joined to 45. The clock comes out of pin 2 on row 52 and reaches both gates through the crossbar. Node X is row 59; rows 10 to 12, 40 to 42 and 60 are empty.

With one BS270 and the BC550C this is the switch block: the BC550C sits on rows 7 to 9 facing the rails, its collector on row 9 behind C4, and rows 11 and 12 are empty. LO on row 19 and LOB on row 13 come from pin 2 and pin 10 of the CD4049.

Step 8, the C3 trim, cannot use the 47 kHz alias trick here, since the clock is not the script's; fit 15 pF, or trim with a scope.

TRACK instead of a knob. The squarer is the other way to set the frequency, and it needs the Jumperless clock, so it belongs with the main drawing, not this one. The two are alternatives: TRACK exists to replace the knob. Its two inverters, pins 3 to 2 and 5 to 4, go where the first tab's squarer drawing puts them, rows 47 to 55, and share the chip with the LOB inverter at 5 V. That changes three things. C3 comes down to about 10 pF, because LOB then swings 5 V while LO swings 3.3 V. The 3.3 V GPIO into pin 9 is below the CD4049's guaranteed 3.5 V input high at 5 V; typical parts switch near 2.5 V and step 1 shows whether yours does, and a 74HCU04 run from 3.3 V removes the question. And the squarer hears the chopper's own clock, which is the hunting the first tab warns about, so the script should count with the LO parked high for a few milliseconds at a time. That last item is a script change for the code session, and untested.

Going to 1 MHz

The window can go to 1 MHz. Three things change, and one of them is the shunt.

What does not change: the mixing and the amplifier. The coil works into a low impedance, so its current is the same for the same field at any frequency, the switch chops current, and the amplifier only ever sees the 1 kHz difference. In simulation the same field gives the same tone at 40 kHz, 200 kHz and 1 MHz: 60, 61 and 62 mV with an ideal switch. (This run also connected the coil's 30 pF self-capacitance, which the earlier decks had left floating; it changes nothing below 100 kHz.)

The oscillator reaches 1 MHz once R10 drops. 22 kΩ against the chip's 15 pF input capacitance was the limit, not C6. With R10 = 2.2 kΩ, R9 = 4.7 kΩ and the 20 kΩ pot, three values of C6 cover the range:

Simulated at 5 V. The top of each band is set by the inverter delays as much as by RC, so expect 30 % spread between chips there. C0G or film for C6; 820 pF or 1 nF pulls the bottom band down to roughly 23 or 19 kHz (scaled, not simulated). Node X swings about 2 V past both rails; R10 at 2.2 kΩ keeps the protection-diode current near half a milliamp.

The gate drive just about keeps up. The CD4049 sources about a milliamp and a BS270 gate takes about a nanocoulomb, so at 1 MHz the rising gate edge is a good fraction of the 500 ns half-cycle. In simulation it still works: 66 mV of tone with RG at 1 kΩ, 69 mV with RG at 100 Ω plus 1 kΩ pull-ups from pins 2 and 10 to 5 V. The cost is headroom. The 1 MHz leak and the charge each edge injects swing the output about ±0.3 V, inaudible but a third of what the amplifier has; a 74HC4066 in place of the two MOSFETs brings that to ±0.09 V. Fit the pull-ups; RG can stay 1 kΩ.

The shunt is where the NPN stops. The BC550C as the shunt is fine to 200 kHz (55 mV), half gone at 500 kHz (27 mV) and dead at 1 MHz (8 mV). That is storage time, not drive: less base current does not help, and in simulation neither does a speed-up capacitor. For the 47 pF band the shunt has to be a MOSFET (a second BS270, a 2N7000, a BS170), a 74HC4066, or a real switching NPN such as a 2N2369 or PN2369 (56 mV at 1 MHz). So: build with the BC550C and use the 680 pF and 150 pF bands; when the 47 pF band goes in, change the shunt too.

The simplest version: one CD4053

The CD4053 in the new kit is a triple SPDT analog switch, and one of its three switches is the whole chopper. The common pin is node A, the Y throw goes to the amplifier through C1, the X throw goes to ground, and the select pin is LO: select high connects the common to Y. That is the series switch, the shunt, the inverter and break-before-make in one package, with no gate resistors, no pull-downs, no C3, no C4, and nothing to orient. The oscillator stays exactly as built; only inverter D goes idle.

What the datasheet costs: 470 Ω on-resistance at 5 V (1050 max) and about 450 ns to switch. The resistance does not matter here. The coil is a current source and the switch chops current, so in simulation the tone is 60 mV at 40 kHz and at 200 kHz, the same as the MOSFETs, with a quarter of their leak at the output. The switching time does matter: at 5 V, about 300 kHz is the comfortable top. For the 47 pF band, feed VEE (pin 7) from DAC 0 at −5 V instead of ground. The chip then runs on its 10 V figures, 180 Ω and 160 ns, and the simulation gives 66 mV at 1 MHz; the select input is still 0 to 5 V logic, so pin 2 of the CD4049 drives it as before.

The rest of the new kit, in one line each:

  • CD4066. Four SPST switches; two make the series-and-shunt pair, but then inverter D is needed again for LOB. The CD4053 is simpler.
  • CD4047. An astable with complementary Q and not-Q at exactly 50 %, f = 1/(4.4 RC). A tidy oscillator for the lower bands; not needed while the CD4049 one works.
  • CD4093. One Schmitt gate, one R and one C make an oscillator, and the other three gates are strong drivers. An alternative to the three-inverter circuit, not a better one.
  • CD4013. A divide-by-two gives exact 50 % and complementary outputs from an oscillator at twice the frequency. Useful with the CD4066, not with the CD4053.
  • CD4060. Oscillator plus 14-stage divider: the hardware DIVIDE of Options A and B, clocked by a squarer.
  • LM393, LM339. Comparators: a squarer for TRACK that runs at 3.3 V and ends the pin 9 question.
  • NE555. No. It does not reach the band cleanly and its duty cycle is not 50 %.
