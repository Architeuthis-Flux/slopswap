Keeping it in step with Photoshop

  1. Press Link artwork and pick the PSD once. While this page stays open it re-reads the file every time you save, and the chip at the top turns green.
  2. If the picker is blocked where you are viewing this, drop the PSD on the box instead. If the chip stays grey after a drop, the browser gave a one-time copy; drop it again after saving.
  3. Each top-level layer or group is sent to view A, view B, both, or neither. The eye icon on top-level layers is ignored, so you can keep hiding one view while you draw the other.
  4. For 3D, set Images per gap to 3 or more. Each layer or group is one depth plane; Split lists a group’s layers separately so each can have its own depth.

Read straight from the PSD: pixel layers, groups, opacity, raster masks, solid colour fills, colour and gradient overlays, strokes, and CMYK colour through the SWOP v2 profile. Anything else is listed under the layers so you know when to drop exported PNGs instead.

What the model assumes

  1. Ridges are opaque black walls with square shoulders. Domes are half-ellipses. The lens sheet is an ideal lens with a little focus blur.
  2. Every process except the first is drawn with your artwork’s colours as if they sat on the raised ink or under the lens. They are there to compare how each mechanism flips, not as printable separations.
  3. The viewer is far enough away that sight lines are parallel. The shirt is a flat sheet or a section of a cylinder.
  4. The 3D views are made by sliding each layer sideways in proportion to its depth. Nothing is redrawn in perspective, and both eyes see the same mix, so the depth reads from movement only.
  5. A view counts as clean when the other image is under 10% of what shows. Ridges only means under 4% of the surface is print.
  6. Colour is the flat artwork. Ink gain, the white underbase and fabric texture are not modelled, so real stripes will be a little softer than these.

Lenticular Shirt Processes

The “lenticular” garment prints I could find work one of four ways. Two put the colour on the raised ink itself, one bonds on a real plastic lens, and one hides part of a flat print behind raised walls. The last is the only one a CMYK + white + one HD screen press can do. It is what your puff test did, and it is the least documented.

Where your shirts stand

Four ways a shirt changes with angle

1 · Walls over a flat print

Two images interlaced flat on the shirt; opaque ridges stand between the stripes. From one side each ridge hides the far stripe. Needs one HD screen.

2 · Colour on the walls

Ribs are built from stacked HD layers. Pieces of one image run up the left wall of every rib, the other up the right wall; the caps mask both head-on. One screen per layer, per colour.

3 · Two-tone domes

A raised line of HD, gel or puff rounds over in the dryer, with a contrasting colour down each flank. You see the flank facing you. Colours are spot inks in tight register.

4 · A real lens, bonded on

An interlaced print under an extruded plastic lens sheet, welded or sewn to the garment. Flips cleanly and holds photo detail, but it is a stiff patch, not a print.

What carries over to this shirt

Art

  • Hidden images have to be simple and bold. Winters and Screenworks both say it, and it matches what went wrong with the board.
  • The two views should differ in outline and in colour where they overlap. The standing figure against the leaping cut-out does that.
  • Design for the ridges from the start. Winters found adding the effect to existing art much harder.

Press

  • Print HD last, with minimal squeegee pressure, one stroke, and about double the usual off-contact. Raise it again for a second hit by the height of the first.
  • HD wants a viscous, short-bodied ink. Cheap HD thins on press after about 100 prints and the lines slump.
  • Cure 325–380°F through the whole deposit, with the belt slowed. Cured HD is matte with sharp edges; gel is glossy and rounded.

Stencil

  • Capillary film comes in 100 to 700 µm (Chromaline Super Phat); 200–400 µm is what most HD work uses.
  • Films of 200 µm and up are adhered with emulsion, not water, then dried for hours or overnight.
  • Expose with metal halide or LED, not fluorescent tubes. Rule of thumb: 90 seconds per 100 µm at 3–6 kW. Soak before washout so the walls stay square.
  • Yellow, low-elongation mesh around 80–110 threads with a thin 70 µm thread.
  • A thick stencil cannot resolve a line finer than the fabric plus the stencil (Ulano). Edges also soften as a single film gets thicker; two thinner hits stay crisper.

Registration

  • Every source calls register the thing that makes or breaks it.
  • In the barrier layout the HD line only has to cover the seam between stripes, so trap the colour under it: let both stripes run to the ridge centre.
  • Keep the T marks from your last file, top and bottom on the centre line, on the HD screen and all four colours.

This widens the gap from the tightest 0.52 mm to 0.75 mm so each stripe is 0.38 mm, which moves the start of the clean view out from 18° to 25° but gives the HD screen more room to land. It is the same 30 px pitch as your puff line file. A single hit through 400 µm at the same pitch does not turn clean until about 43°, further round than most people will stand.

Print a strip first: the same art at three gaps (0.6, 0.75 and 0.9 mm) with one and two HD hits, then check it on a body, not a table. Across a chest the 10.6 in wide art spans roughly 34°, so one viewer sees different views on its left and right edges. A lenticular area nearer 6 in wide flips as a whole.

Stripes 0.38 mm wide are narrower than a 45 LPI halftone cell (0.56 mm). Keep the fills under the ridges solid process builds, as the rainbow bands already are, and save the visible halftone for the wordmark and anything outside the ridges.
